What “no-vig” means

A sportsbook’s prices on both sides of a market add up to more than 100% implied probability. At −110 on each side, each price implies 52.38%; together 104.76%. The extra 4.76% is the overround — the book’s margin, often called the vig or juice. Removing it gives a no-vig (fair) probability for each side: the market’s own estimate with the margin taken out.

A no-vig price is a benchmark, not a prediction. It tells you what the market implies; it does not tell you who will win, and it is only as good as the market you entered.

The four methods

Every method agrees on the total margin. They differ on how it is split between the favourite and the underdog, which matters most on lopsided markets.

MethodFormulaWhen it differs
Multiplicativepᵢ = qᵢ ÷ ΣqThe common default. Removes margin in proportion to each price.
Powerpᵢ = qᵢᵏ, Σ qᵢᵏ = 1Takes more margin from the longshot (favourite–longshot bias).
Shininsider share z solved so Σp = 1Also leans on the longshot; on a two-way market it equals the additive method.
Additivepᵢ = qᵢ − (Σq − 1) ÷ nEqual margin per side; can fail on a heavy longshot (the tool says so).

q is a price’s implied probability (1 ÷ decimal odds). EdgeDesk’s terminal uses the same four methods, from the same arithmetic, as its market benchmark — never as EdgeDesk’s own probability.

Questions

Should I enter both sides of the same market?

Yes — every outcome of one market at one book (a spread’s two sides at the same number, a moneyline’s two sides, or a three-way market’s three outcomes). Mixing books or numbers makes the “margin” meaningless; the tool refuses totals under 98% or over 150%.

Is the fair price a good bet?

Not by itself. A fair price is the market’s own number with the margin removed. Whether a bet has value depends on your probability against the price you can actually get — the fair odds calculator does that arithmetic for a probability you supply.
