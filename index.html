    ▪ Three separate checks

    The price. The model. The books.
    Never blended.

    Each one answers a different question, from a different source. Each one is graded on its own.

    The price

    Is this sportsbook charging less than everyone else?

    EdgeDesk takes the house margin out of every sportsbook’s number, anchors to the book whose prices move fastest, and compares. Then it grades itself on where the price finished before kickoff.

    The model

    Does EdgeDesk’s own model see the game differently?

    A separate football engine that projects the game from scratch. Treated as experimental until its published record earns it more than that.

    The books

    Do the sportsbooks disagree with each other?

    How far apart the books are on the same game. That is a measure of how settled the number is — not a prediction of anything.

    These three are kept apart on purpose. EdgeDesk never combines them into a single score, and never lets one borrow confidence from another, because agreement manufactured from unrelated systems is not evidence.

    The only scoreboard that isn't luck

    Winning on Saturday doesn’t prove your process works. Did you get the better price?

    The idea in one line: just before a game starts, every sportsbook has settled on its final number. If the price you took was better than that final number, you bought well — whatever happened in the game.

    That final number is the market's most accurate price, and beating it is the only signal that exists before results do. Bettors call it closing line value. If you consistently bought a better number than the market settled on, your process is finding something. If you didn’t, a winning Saturday was luck wearing a disguise.
